# Alex the Locksmith — Chapter 1 Draft: The Ad That Changed Everything

> **Note:** This draft is based on Interview Batch 1 (2026-02-13). Dates/locations are intentionally kept a little fuzzy until Batch 2 confirms timeline specifics.

I didn’t grow up as “the locksmith guy.”

If anything, I started the opposite way: at rock bottom.

When I answered that locksmith ad, I barely knew tools. I’m not exaggerating. I hadn’t used a drill. I was the kind of guy who could maybe handle a screwdriver if you pointed me at the right screw. I was job-hunting, trying to get my footing, trying to figure out what I could do that would actually pay the bills.

Then I found an ad.

“Locksmith.”

To me, it sounded simple: show up, open a door, help someone, get paid. Easy.

The first surprise was how fast they throw you into the fire.

There’s no gentle training montage. No patient teacher standing over your shoulder. You’re on calls, you’re in driveways, you’re in parking lots at weird hours, and the customer is staring at you like you’re Superman and a surgeon rolled into one.

And you’re thinking, *I don’t even know what I’m doing yet.*

But the bigger surprise—one I didn’t fully understand at first—was that I hadn’t just joined a trade.

I had stepped into an entire world.

## The Scam I Didn’t Know I Joined

What most people don’t realize is that locksmithing has a scam problem. A big one.

There are companies that aren’t really locksmith companies in the way you’d think. They’re lead machines. They flood Google with fake listings, fake names, and fake “local” pages. They run ads everywhere. They answer the phone like they’re down the street, but they’re not. Sometimes they’re not even in the country.

And the person they send out to you? That’s the “locksmith.” That’s the guy in the parking lot trying to figure out how to do the job while also trying not to get blamed for a system he didn’t build.

That was the trap.

At first, I didn’t have the full picture. I was just trying to learn. Trying to survive. Trying to not look stupid while someone’s family is waiting on the porch.

But it didn’t take long to notice the patterns.

The way the calls came in.

The way the pricing went.

The way the “training” wasn’t about skill.

It was about getting paid.

I’ll never forget something that sums it up perfectly—something I heard that felt like it belonged in a different universe:

> “We don’t pay you to pick locks… we pay you to drill and charge as much as possible.”

That’s not locksmithing.

That’s predation.

A real locksmith tries to be non-destructive first. You pick first. You manipulate. You look for the clean solution. Drilling is a last resort because drilling destroys hardware, costs the customer more, and turns a bad day into a bigger repair.

In the scam world, drilling is a feature: it’s fast, it’s dramatic, and it justifies a bigger bill.

The worst part is how it traps beginners. When you don’t know what “normal” looks like, you keep running calls and telling yourself this must be how the business works.

## The Night in Mount Dora

There was a moment—one call—that snapped something in my head.

It was late. Like 2:00 AM late.

Mount Dora.

The customer was locked out, and they were the rightful homeowner. This wasn’t shady. This was someone stuck outside their own place, in the dark, needing help.

I did what I thought I had to do. I drilled and replaced the lock.

In the moment, you’re focused on solving the problem. You’re thinking about the steps. The drill. The hardware. The replacement. The goal is to get the person inside.

Then the neighbors called the cops.

I didn’t even know that part until I was leaving.

I’m driving away, and I look up and see what looks like a scene from a movie: helicopter, multiple police cars. My first thought wasn’t even, *They’re coming for me.* It was more like, *Somebody’s in trouble. Something happened.*

And then I got pulled over.

Here’s the thing: if you’ve never been on the wrong side of flashing lights at 2:00 AM, let me tell you what happens in your head.

Your heart starts writing stories.

Bad stories.

And in that moment, I realized how exposed I was.

Because I was alone.

The “company” that sent me out? They didn’t answer. No support. No backup. No one to confirm who I was or why I was there.

Just me.

Luckily, the cops were cool.

They saw the business card. They saw the shirt I had made. They could tell I wasn’t out there trying to break into a house for fun.

But the fear didn’t disappear just because it ended okay.

It did something else.

It made me understand what could have happened.

If those cops weren’t cool—if the situation had escalated—my life could have gone sideways fast.

And I would have been left “out to dry” by the very people who were making money off my labor.

That was the night I decided I couldn’t keep doing it their way.

## The First Step Toward Being Legit

A lot of people assume the big change is some dramatic announcement.

But for me, the exit started with something simple and personal: I began making my own uniforms.

I’ve made shirts for years. It’s one of those skills you pick up and keep because it’s useful and it feels like control. When you’ve been at rock bottom, control matters.

So I made locksmith shirts. Real-looking, clean, professional.

It might sound small, but it was a statement.

If I was going to be out at night helping people, I wanted to look like someone they could trust.

I wanted to show up as a real business.

Even before I had the full business.

Because here’s what I learned fast: a locksmith isn’t just opening doors. You’re showing up in people’s worst moments, and you can either help—or take advantage.

That question became my compass.

My rule—still my rule—is simple: treat customers like you’d treat your own family. It’s the exact opposite of scam training.

## Winter Park Roadside: Learning the Real Trade

When I finally quit, I didn’t quit to become “rich.” I quit to become real.

I started my own thing and focused on emergency lockouts—honest problems, urgent moments. I wanted to learn the trade the right way and leave people safer than I found them.

That night in Mount Dora was scary, but it made the choice obvious. From then on, I wasn’t going to be somebody’s disposable “tech.” I was going to build something local and honest—something I could stand behind.
